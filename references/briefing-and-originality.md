# Briefing and originality

Read this before substantial production or when an edit request leaves the intended treatment unclear.

## Ask until the material choices are settled

Start from the user's existing instructions, files, references, and approvals. Record each material item as **user-specified**, **explicitly delegated**, **answered by evidence**, or **open**. A reasonable production judgment is not permission to invent a preference the user said they wanted to choose.

Ask in small rounds, normally two or three related questions, in plain language. Offer a recommendation and understandable alternatives where helpful. An answer may resolve several items at once. Avoid re-asking questions already answered in the session. Let the user delegate creative decisions; that is a valid resolution, not a missing answer.

First clarify the purpose and treatment. Then clarify presentation and constraints. Do not ask someone to choose a font size before knowing what kind of video they want.

| Area | Learn enough to decide |
| --- | --- |
| Subject and goal | Exact topic, scope, what the viewer should learn/feel/do, required points and exclusions |
| Viewer | Age/experience where relevant, language, assumed knowledge, and technical depth |
| Treatment | Documentary, tutorial, essay, animation, comedy, interview, review, narrative fiction, or a user-defined mix |
| Tone | Serious, curious, playful, dramatic, calm, skeptical; degree of humor and intensity |
| Presentation | Narrated, host-led, interview-led, silent/visual, or mixed; desired voice/accent/pronunciation when applicable |
| Visuals | Preferred source types, realism/stylization, movement, necessary demonstrations, brand constraints if any |
| Text | Captions on/off and treatment, explanatory labels, language/accessibility needs, unwanted overlays |
| Sound | Music genre/style, BPM, song key, drums, instruments, mood/intensity, sound effects, when quiet is purposeful, narration priority if present |
| References | What specific qualities to learn from examples, plus things to avoid; do not clone another creator's identity |
| Resources | Available footage/assets/accounts, authorized spending cap, time constraints, tool/model restrictions |
| Output | Default 10–12 minutes; requested resolution/orientation, review destination, ancillary deliverables and publication scope |

Do not require all fields for every genre. For example, a silent animation needs no narrator choice. A channel is optional. For standalone work, retain only an episode brief; create or update a reusable channel profile only when requested.

When examples are absent, describe two or three treatment options and their consequences. Use low-cost sketches or existing assets where useful; do not purchase samples before spending is authorized. If an answer is required, keep dependent work pending. Elapsed time is not an answer.

## The agreed direction protects the video

Summarize the viewer promise, scope, tone, treatment, length, must-keep elements, must-avoid elements, and delegated choices. Cite the user's instructions that establish them. The director can resolve routine implementation details inside this direction. A change to the central promise, audience, genre, narrator, or explicitly protected element needs user direction unless that change was already delegated.

Research can expose a false premise. The crew should flag it with sources and propose a truthful alternative; the director must not preserve a false factual claim merely to protect a story outline. Ask the user when correcting the premise materially changes the requested video.

## Check the opening promise and claim scope

Review the first 15 seconds from the intended viewer's position: **What is this about? Why should I care? What will watching resolve or help me do?** Picture and sound can answer together; no spoken agenda is required. Study how supplied successful references establish interest, recording observed intervals, without copying their wording or assuming retention data.

A historical-mission hook can establish the objective and an extreme obstacle while withholding the outcome. A provider explainer can establish the shared problem, why these companies illustrate it, and what viewers will learn. These are applications of the same promise check, not mandatory hook templates or a requirement to finish the entire opening by 15 seconds.

Apply the group-scope rule only when making a group claim. A video about five companies need not rank them or show all five in every shot. But a total described as covering those five must include their relevant figures or identify exclusions. If one included round is from an earlier year, the total's date label must reflect that; a narrower period needs an explicitly labeled subset. Never fill missing data by guessing or add unlike measures such as funding, valuation and revenue. Record membership, measure, date scope and arithmetic beside the claim.

## Reusable materials should preserve method, not sameness

Reuse the workbook structure, media-handling routines, review process, and configurable rendering tools. Fill them with new decisions and evidence for every episode. Do not reuse a prior script, palette, narrator, music cue, shot order, pacing curve, or ending composition unless that reuse fits the request or an agreed channel identity.

For each treatment, write a short explanation of why its choices fit this subject and viewer. Compare against available relevant prior work for repeated opening devices, stock metaphors, visual families, clips, music, and endings. Reuse can be purposeful—such as revisiting a mechanism with new information—but must add understanding. Changing a crop or heading alone does not make repeated filler fresh.

Do not impose an arbitrary percentage of "new" footage. A tutorial may need repeated views of the same interface; an interview may stay with one speaker; an animation may contain no live footage. The test is whether the viewer is receiving meaningful development.

Examples of applying the same quality method differently:

- **Cooking tutorial:** overhead demonstrations, readable quantities, room for an action to finish, clear practical steps, and kitchen sound. A diagram appears only if useful.
- **Animated science explanation:** stylized models, staged visual comparisons, consistent scale, and carefully synchronized explanations. Graphics can carry the entire video.
- **Historical documentary:** verified archival/context footage, supported claims, purposeful reconstructions, and a narrative suited to the evidence.

These are explanations, not mandatory presets or new topics to pursue.

## Where evidence comes from

| Question | Evidence to obtain |
| --- | --- |
| What does the user want? | Current conversation, supplied brief, approved choices, and explicitly delegated decisions |
| What does a reference actually do? | Direct inspection of accessible media; record the viewed interval and what was observed |
| Is a factual claim supported? | Primary documents/data/recordings where possible, authoritative explanations, and appropriate corroboration |
| May a media asset be used this way? | Asset-specific license, source policy, permission, and intended-use conditions; a search result is not clearance |
| Is a voice consistent? | Actual provider voice identifier, model/settings, pronunciation record, and an approved audio reference |
| Does the edit work? | Actual current-cut audio/motion inspection, the script/beat plan, and precise observations |
| What did production cost? | Provider request IDs, quotes/reservations, billing events, refunds, included-credit use, and local records |
| Did delivery succeed? | The exact delivered artifact and playback/import/upload state at the authorized destination |

Separate source evidence from reviewer interpretation. Generated content, a model's confident prose, and a script are not independent proof of a historical or technical claim. Unknown facts and inaccessible references remain explicitly uncertain. Do not claim to have watched, heard, researched, or paid for something without evidence.
